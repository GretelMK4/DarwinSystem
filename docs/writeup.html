Defensive deception against AI attack swarms · concept and simulations

The Darwin System

Three traps modeled on carnivorous plants, wired together so each one makes the others stronger. They lure automated attackers in, keep them busy with convincing fakes, and warn people early.

The idea

Trap the attacker instead of fighting back

Hacking an attacker back is illegal for private defenders and risks hitting innocent machines that attackers hijack. Darwin never touches the attacker's systems. Everything happens on infrastructure you own: you make your own network expensive, confusing and dangerous to attack.

The design rests on one observation. Some things on a network have no legitimate users at all: a hidden app, a decoy server, a fake controller. Anything that touches them is almost certainly an attacker. That gives an alarm with very few false positives, and it gives attackers something worth wasting their time on.

How they connect

The ecosystem is the point

In testing, each plant alone was beatable, and once Hercules made Judith worse by distracting the attackers that would have tripped her alarm. Connected, they cover each other's blind spots.

Lure

Nectar a bot can't resist: leaked-looking configs, juicy names in public certificate logs, hidden decoy apps, decoy controllers listed inside real engineering documents.

Hold

Endless fakes generated on the fly. Cheap for the defender, expensive for an attacker that pays for every step it takes.

Poison

Sealed bots get believable fake data, so even a successful theft is mostly worthless. In one test, 99.8% of what a swarm took home was fake.

Warn

Every touch of a decoy is a near-certain signal. Signals from different plants combine into a lockdown and an alert people can act on quickly.

How it evolved

Fourteen rounds of attack and fix

Each round was a simulation on local test servers. Most rounds ended with the attackers finding a weakness and the next round fixing it.

  1. Tarpit mazeJudith, first versionA greedy bot spent 99% of its requests inside and dug 69 folders deep. A loop-detecting bot escaped in 4.8 s until folder names were generated from word combinations; then it stayed trapped for the full test.
  2. Fake API layerJudithA bot that tests stolen keys escaped instantly when 3 keys failed. With a fake API that accepts the trap's own keys, it stayed the full 25 s, "stole" 1,860 fake records and climbed 22 levels of fake privilege.
  3. One-way sealJudithPast a set depth, even the real homepage turns fake for that bot. A bot that fled after being sealed saw trap pages on 6 of its 9 "escape" pages.
  4. Nectar on a fake internetJudith + lures40 aimless bots, 61 sites. With a juicy name and a leaked config, the share of bots going to the trap first rose from 5% to 81%, and the real sites took 27% fewer attack requests.
  5. Ant pheromone scoutsJudithLetting every 4th bot leave with "gold" made the swarm recruit itself: 37 of 60 bots followed the trail in. A cautious swarm that avoided a plain trap (1 recruit) sent 16.
  6. Catch and releaseJudithReleasing trapped bots after 8 s burned 40% more attacker time against a cautious swarm. Releasing after 4 s backfired, and releasing at all hurt against naive swarms.
  7. Multi-pitcher lockdownJudith150 bots against a 3,000-item catalog. A standard rate limit lost everything. Five pitchers plus lockdown and the shadow shield cut real theft to 25%, with the swarm's haul 75% fake.
  8. Phone flytrapVenusOne decoy touch (with the phone's own scanner trusted) cut sensitive leaks from about 11 of 15 files to 1, and caught a slow agent that a 3-touches-in-60-s rule missed entirely.
  9. Hidden decoy apps and kill switchVenusThe only defense that caught deep spyware: leaks fell from 21 of 21 to 5. "Alive" decoys made the kill switch fire 89% of the time versus 52%. No false kill in 2,000 normal weeks.
  10. Full ecosystemall three + sharing300 smart bots. Sharing cut phone leaks from 17% to 1.6%. Hercules sites built to look different held 11% of the swarm's effort instead of 0.6%.
  11. A smarter swarm breaks itstress test600 bots that never touch pitchers on their target and test stolen keys across sites took 100% of the catalog and exposed every Hercules site.
  12. Defense v2fixesHidden honey links, a surge alarm, separate key registries per Hercules site and malware fingerprint sharing brought theft back down to 23%. A browser-like swarm that skipped the hidden links still took 100%: it raised only two kinds of alarm and this round needed three. With the threshold lowered to two it took 36%.
  13. Defense v3fixesAn instant traffic tripwire, an enumeration detector, an entry puzzle and a busy cap took every swarm under 10%, including one that knew every threshold.
  14. Critical infrastructureabstract modelA utility-style network with a control system. Darwin stopped the fast and patient attackers almost every time and warned people hours ahead; careful attackers with real logins got through about a third to half the time.

Critical infrastructure

Could it protect something important?

An abstract model of a utility-style network: corporate IT, one guarded door into the control network, and 30 controllers running physical equipment. Conventional security was always on. The question was whether Darwin stops an attacker, or at least warns people in time. 1,000 simulated attacks per case.

AttackerConventional only: reached a controllerDarwin (dense)Warning to humans when stopped
Blitz: fast, noisy100%0%90% flagged surveying the sector before breaking in; alarm within ~2 min of entry
Stealth: patient, mostly passive96%1.7%First alert ~80 min after entry; such attacks needed ~4 h to reach a controller
Deception-aware: avoids bait99%46%Alert ~2.5 h in, against ~4.5 h needed
Stolen engineer logins100%37%Alert ~26 min in, against ~54 min needed

The limit, stated plainly. When a careful attacker did get through, humans were warned beforehand only about 3% of the time. An attacker holding real logins that ignores bait leaves almost nothing for deception to catch. In a real facility Darwin belongs beside controls that don't depend on deception, such as logins that can't be phished and two-person approval for commands to equipment.

Cost to normal operations: about 2 false alerts a week, mostly people opening a decoy file by accident, and roughly one false lockdown every 7 months, delaying engineers about 15 minutes.

Lessons

What held up in every round

Zero-user decoys are near-perfect alarms

Hidden apps, decoy servers and decoy controllers almost never fired on innocent people. Normal visitors saw zero fake pages across every web test.

Connection beats any single trap

Sharing intelligence was the largest single improvement. Unconnected, one plant can even weaken another.

Every single trigger is a weak point

Smart swarms beat each alarm by avoiding its one trigger. What worked was putting traps where the attacker must step: in the scraper's own path, in traffic volume, in the documents it reads.

Context changes the rules

On a website, waiting for two different signals avoids false alarms. Inside a control network, a single touch of a decoy should lock the door.

The real win is time

Against the smartest attackers, Darwin didn't build a wall. It turned attacks that took under a second into ones that took most of an hour, and gave people warning during that time.

Fakes must cost real users nothing

One rule (a fixed product budget for new visitors) fed real shoppers fake pages during an attack and barely slowed the swarm. It was removed. Every rule was tested against simulated people too.

Limits

How far to trust these results

  • Both sides were written by the same team. The attackers could only be as clever as we thought to make them. Each new attacker tactic broke something, so a real adversary will find cracks these tests never imagined.
  • Time was compressed. Real internet sweeps take hours or days. That likely understates Hercules's early-warning value, and it caps how much a patient attacker could steal within a 60-second test.
  • Thresholds were tuned to simulated traffic. A real shop or plant would set them from its own baseline, and a real flash sale could trip the traffic tripwire.
  • Few repeats. Most web results average two runs; the phone and infrastructure models ran 500–1,000 trials each.
  • The building blocks are not new. Honeytokens, tarpits, decoy networks and crawler mazes exist today. The ecosystem framing and several mechanisms here (scout release, one-way seal, the shadow shield, hidden decoy apps on phones) are not something we've seen packaged together, though no literature search has been done.
  • Rules that stay fixed: decoys use fictional brands only, everything runs on infrastructure the defender owns, nothing ever attacks back, and testing on real critical systems requires an authorized red-team exercise.
Next steps

From simulation to evidence

Break the self-grading

Have an attacker built by someone who hasn't seen the defense, such as a separate AI agent working blind.

Meet real bots

Run Hercules and Judith on a cheap, isolated cloud server for a few weeks and record what actually shows up.

Prototype Venus

One hidden decoy app plus an on-device VPN watcher on Android, to learn what the platform really allows.

Watch the long game

Detection over hours: spotting a "crowd" that is systematically working through an entire catalog or network.
